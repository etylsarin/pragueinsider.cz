City representatives put a figure on Prague's proposed orbital metro on 2 September: built with
standard tunnels, line E would cost an estimated CZK 220 billion. Councillors are due to approve the
commissioning of a feasibility study next week, and it is that study — not the estimate — that will
establish what the line actually is.

The proposed route runs from Smíchov via Pankrác, Bohdalec, Žižkov, Vysočany and Letňany to the
planned northern terminal of the high-speed railway. At roughly 22 kilometres it would enlarge
Prague's metro network by 29 percent, which is the scale that makes the price plausible rather than
alarming.

## What the study is for

The Institute of Planning and Development will prepare it, following on from a first analysis the
institute has already done. Deputy mayor for transport Jaromír Beránek (Pirates) said the study will
examine the placement of stations, how they tie into existing and future development, interchange
relationships, economic demands, and the possible use of different types of rail transport on
individual sections. Beránek put the work at a year to a year and a half once councillors commission
it.

That last item is the one to watch. The line would not have to run underground for its whole length,
which is directly connected to what the whole project costs — and the study is to address that too.
An orbital route through Bohdalec and Vysočany crosses a good deal of ground where a surface or
elevated alignment is at least arguable.

The councillors are also to commission a manual for using the development potential of the existing
metro stations and their surroundings — a separate document, and a signal that the city is treating
station catchments as a planning asset rather than as leftover space.

## An idea from the 1980s, and a corridor now protected

Deputy mayor Petr Hlaváček (STAN) traced the idea of a metro line connecting parts of the city
outside the centre to the 1980s. What is new is that the reservation for its route is now written
into the Metropolitan Plan, the zoning document that took effect on Tuesday. A corridor in a binding
plan is not a decision to build, but it is the thing that stops the route being built over while the
city argues about it.

The stated purpose is to get people out of cars and onto public transport, relieve the centre, fill
in the direct fast links between outer districts that are currently missing or inadequate, and serve
areas where more residential development is expected.

Tomáš Portlík (ODS), chair of the Spolu group of councillors, set the condition plainly:

> The costs must be such that the city is able to pay them.
>
> — Tomáš Portlík, chair of the Spolu group of Prague city councillors

## Where the ring stops being a metro

The scheme was first pushed in the previous term by Adam Scheinherr (Praha Sobě), then deputy mayor
for transport and now an opposition councillor. He identified the eastern section as the priority,
but his proposal also envisaged eventually closing the whole circle via Bohnice, Podbaba and Strahov
back to Smíchov.

Beránek said a tram would be enough for that western half, served by the new Bohnice–Podbaba line.
Capacity there could be raised, he suggested, by lengthening platforms so that longer sets can call
at them, or by raising the permitted speed. This is the substantive difference between the two
proposals: whether the ring is a metro all the way round, or a metro on the east and a tram on the
west.

## One date that does not add up

ČTK reports Beránek as saying construction should follow the completion of line D between náměstí
Míru and Písnice as quickly as possible, and gives that line's completion, on the current schedule,
as the start of 2026. That does not square with the schedule the city gave for the same line at the
end of August, when the first operating section, Pankrác to Nové Dvory, was put at the second half
of 2032. We report both dates as they were published rather than reconcile them.
