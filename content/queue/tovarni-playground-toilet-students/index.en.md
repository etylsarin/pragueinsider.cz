The children's playground on Tovární street in Holešovice has a new step-free public toilet. It
is not the catalogue cabin a municipal district usually buys. The larch-clad structure was
designed and built by students of Ateliér Mádr at the Czech Technical University's Faculty of
Architecture, working with the Prague 7 district council. It has a changing table and is open
from April to October between 8am and 6pm.

On Tuesday 22 September at 4pm the students and their teachers meet on the playground. The
council says the gathering "concludes a successful collaboration on the creation of the new
toilet in this place", and every student design for the toilet will be exhibited there — not
only the one that got built.

## A house inside a house

The built scheme is by Klára Klingová and Viktor Ondřích, and the faculty describes its
principle as a house within a house: an inner volume holds the lavatory itself, while an outer
skin of vertical larch battens shades and protects the structure and, at one point, turns into
a bicycle stand.

That last move is the substantial one. A public toilet on a playground is usually a foreign
body — a locked cell nobody wants in view. Here the skin is also a piece of street furniture,
so the object does two jobs at once and does not have to be hidden in a corner of the site.

According to the faculty the structure was assembled in front of the school building and
delivered to the playground finished; it has been in use since September. Josef Mádr and Štěpán
Tomše supervised the work.

## Why it is worth recording

Prague 7 has been fitting toilets to its playgrounds for some time, and one WC would not be
news by itself. The brief is what makes it one. The district did not procure a product; it
procured a prototype drawn, and then actually built, by people who are still studying
architecture.

Design-build is a regular part of architectural teaching. It rarely ends in a structure that
enters a district council's operating schedule and gets cleaned eight months of the year.

Neither the council nor the faculty says what the object cost, who paid for it, or how long it
took to build. That is normal for a commission of this size and it is also the one thing still
worth knowing: without a price there is no way to say whether a student prototype comes in
under a series-built cabin, or over it and better.

For a reader outside Prague 7 the model matters more than the building. The district gets a
small, authored object in public space at low risk; the faculty gets something to offer
students beyond paper. How many commissions of this size Prague hands to practices each year
without any of it touching a school is a question the Tovární toilet puts more plainly than
most conferences on the subject.
