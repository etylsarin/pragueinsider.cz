Prague's city assembly voted late on Thursday night, 10 September, to lend Praha 7 the money it was
short of. The district gets 165.8 million crowns as a repayable advance towards a new primary school
on Jankovcova street in Holešovice, and will pay it back in fifteen annual instalments starting in
2030. ČTK reported the decision.

The loan is the last piece of a financing package the district settled with the city hall in June.
Of the 903 million crowns the school is estimated to cost, the magistrate covers 60 percent as a
grant, another 20 percent is this interest-free loan, and Praha 7 finds the remaining fifth itself.
Martin Tománek (Praha Sobě), the Praha 7 councillor, set out that split when the agreement was
reached in June. The contractor tender went out in July and work is meant to start this autumn.

Zdeněk Kovářík (ODS), the Prague councillor who handles the city's finances, used the vote to argue
for the instrument rather than the building.

> A repayable financial advance is a tool that makes sense to me. This is exactly what responsible
> financial cooperation between the capital and its districts should look like.
>
> — Zdeněk Kovářík, councillor of the City of Prague

## What gets built

The school will stand on the block between Jankovcova, U Vody and Varhulíkové, close to the left
bank of the Vltava — on the line of the former Přádelní street, which the district intends to bring
back so that Jankovcova connects through to the embankment. That is a street being reopened as a
by-product of a school, in a quarter where the grid was broken up by industry and has been slow to
knit back together.

The building has six floors above ground and one below, with 18 classrooms for roughly 550 pupils.
There are outdoor sports facilities, a garden, a large-capacity kitchen and a canteen that doubles
as an assembly hall, plus a playing field on the roof. It takes the name of Jan Vodňanský, the
Holešovice writer, songwriter, poet and man of the theatre.

## The arithmetic behind the loan

Praha 7 runs six primary schools with a combined capacity of 3,370 places, and ČTK reports they are
full to the last one. The district's own demographic study expects 4,500 children aged between six
and fourteen to be living in Praha 7 by 2029. The district has about 48,000 inhabitants across
Holešovice, Bubny and parts of Bubeneč and Libeň.

Set those figures side by side — this is arithmetic on the district's own numbers, not a claim the
sources make — and 550 new places take the total to 3,920 against a cohort of 4,500. The school
closes most of the gap that opens by 2029. It does not close all of it, and it is the only one being
financed.

The cost works out at about 1.64 million crowns per pupil place. That is the kind of figure that
tends to be quoted back at a city when the next district asks for the same deal, and Kovářík's
framing of the advance as a model for how the capital should work with its městské části is an
invitation to exactly that conversation. The advance is repayable; the grant that covers three fifths
of the building is not.

What the decision settles is the money. What it does not settle is the schedule: the tender was
announced in July and the works are only due to begin in the autumn, which means the first cohort is
still several years of construction away from a building that the district's demographers say is
needed by the end of the decade.
