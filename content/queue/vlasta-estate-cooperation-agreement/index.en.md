Prague 10 signed a cooperation agreement on Tuesday 29 September with six owners' associations from
the Vlasta estate, the housing that surrounds the district's own former town hall and the Eden
cultural centre. Both buildings, and the land with them, have been sold. The agreement is about what
the neighbours get to say when the buyer starts designing.

The document sets out how the district and representatives of the surrounding blocks will work
together while the project is prepared and built. Prague 10 says it is ready to negotiate residents'
requirements with the future owner of the site — specifically pedestrian connections, greenery,
parking and the form of the adjoining public spaces. The owners' associations are also to take part
in preparing the urban-architectural competition.

## What was sold, and for how much

In June the Prague 10 assembly approved the sale of the dilapidated former seat of the town hall, the
Eden cultural centre and the related plots to EPRE Vršovická, which offered 1.117 billion crowns. The
district is now continuing the steps needed to acquire a new town hall of its own, and the money from
the sale is earmarked for exactly that.

So the sequence runs: sell the old building because it is beyond saving and the district needs a
usable headquarters; buy a new one with the proceeds; and in between, try to shape what replaces the
old one. The agreement signed in September belongs to the third of those, which is the part the
district has least formal control over, because it no longer owns the land.

## Who is bound by it, and who is not

Read carefully, the agreement commits the district and the owners' associations to each other. The
buyer is not a party to it. What Prague 10 undertakes is to carry residents' requirements into
negotiations with the future owner and to bring the associations into the preparation of the
competition — both real, both procedural. Neither obliges EPRE Vršovická to accept a pedestrian route
or a tree.

That is not a criticism of the document so much as a description of what a district can still do
after a sale. The leverage that remains is the planning process and the competition brief, and the
agreement is an attempt to pre-commit the district to using it in a particular direction, with named
partners who can hold it to that. Six associations with a signed paper are harder to leave out of a
competition brief than six associations without one.

## Why an estate, and not just a building

The phrase in the district's announcement is the area *around* the former town hall, and that is the
right scale for this site. The old town hall and the Eden cultural centre sit inside the Vlasta
estate, a late-socialist housing scheme whose public space was laid out for the buildings that stood
there — paths that cut through, greenery that belongs to no one block, parking that was sized for
different car ownership. Replace two large buildings in the middle of that and every one of those
things changes, whether or not the new scheme touches a square metre outside its own plot.

The four things the district lists are precisely the four that cross the plot boundary. Pedestrian
connections, greenery, parking, adjoining public space: none of them is settled by a building permit
for a new block, and all of them are decided by how the competition brief is written.

## What is not yet known

The competition. Nothing in the announcement gives a date for launching it, a brief, or the mix the
site is to carry. The district also refers to the *future* owner, which suggests the June sale has not
yet completed, and the agreement names no mechanism for what happens if the owner declines to engage.
The next thing worth watching for is the competition brief, because that is where the four words
*pedestrian connections, greenery, parking and public space* either acquire requirements or stay
aspirations.
