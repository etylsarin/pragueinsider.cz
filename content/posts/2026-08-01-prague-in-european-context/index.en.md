The city's planning institute has updated *Praha v evropském kontextu*, its running comparison of
Prague against eleven European peer cities across housing, transport, work, education and leisure.
The picture it produces is unusually stark: on one metric Prague is close to the best in Europe, and
on another it is the worst in the set.

## The worst number

Prague has overtaken every comparison city on the unaffordability of owner-occupied housing.
Buying an apartment costs more than thirteen gross annual salaries — putting Prague level with
Barcelona, Munich and Amsterdam, cities with considerably higher incomes.

Renting offers no relief. A 60-square-metre apartment consumes 40 per cent of the average Prague
salary. The equivalent figure is 28 per cent in Munich and 14 per cent in Vienna. Vienna is the
comparison that should sting: a city of similar size and history where a century of municipal
housing policy produced a rental market costing residents roughly a third of what Praguers pay.

## The income side of the same equation

Unemployment in Prague is among the lowest in Europe, but real incomes lag. A worker in Munich
earns roughly one and a half times what a Prague counterpart does. Copenhagen shows steady income
growth over the period; Munich has stagnated.

Working hours have shortened — Praguers now put in just under 39 hours a week — though that remains
about three hours more than in Western Europe.

## What Prague got right

Public transport is the institute's clearest success story, and the numbers are genuinely strong. A
monthly pass costs under one per cent of the average income. Seven in ten Praguers use the system
regularly and 87 per cent report being satisfied with it. Measured per head of population, Prague
runs one of the longest tram networks in Europe, and its transit is the second most accessible in
the comparison after Barcelona.

## And the part that makes no sense

Despite all of that, Praguers drive. Prague, Warsaw and Bucharest are the only cities in the set
where the number of registered cars is markedly increasing. Stockholm, Copenhagen and Vienna
operate at between a third and a half of Prague's rate of car ownership.

The institute is direct about the consequences: congestion, collisions, public space given over to
parking, and worse air. It is also the finding most obviously connected to the housing numbers
above. When the affordable housing sits at the edge of the metropolitan area and the well-served
transit sits in the centre, car ownership stops being a preference and becomes a workaround. The
report treats housing and transport as separate chapters. Read together, they look like one problem.
