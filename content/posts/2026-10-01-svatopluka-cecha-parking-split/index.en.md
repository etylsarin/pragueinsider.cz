Prague 10 published on 30 September the record of the second public hearing on the winning design
for náměstí Svatopluka Čecha, held on 27 August in the Sál bratří Čapků with about forty people in
the room. Two numbers in it are worth putting side by side. The scheme by ra15 a.s. cuts parking on
the square by roughly 15 percent against today. And when the evening's facilitator asked who would
support a deeper cut than that, something over a third of those present put a hand up — six of them
residents of the square itself.

That is the opposite of how these meetings usually run. Marek Sivák of Architekti prostoru, who
wrote the competition brief and chaired the evening, said as much in the record: the demand that
normally dominates a consultation on a public space is for parking to be kept or expanded, and here
a significant part of the 656 respondents to the earlier survey wanted it reduced instead. The
answers of people living on the square or immediately around it were tracked separately, and even
in that group the wish to keep the present extent of parking did not predominate.

## What the district says is stopping it

Mayor Martin Valovič told the meeting he could personally imagine a sharper reduction in car traffic
on the square, and then set out why the design does not contain one. Participation also produced
demands to keep capacity; the 15 percent figure reflects the degree of change the survey showed
support for. Anything more, he said, would need a wider systemic solution — flexible pricing in the
paid parking zones, which Prague 10 has long argued for and which the city has not found the
political support to adopt.

Milan Maršálek, the councillor for the environment, confirmed that changes to the zone system are
being prepared at city level, including unified rules and time regimes across districts and more
flexible pricing, but that they have not been approved and will probably fall to the next electoral
term.

One participant was not persuaded. The record has him calling it an alibi — a row of speakers from
the district and the design team all conceding they could imagine less parking, while no sharper
change is actually proposed — and warning that a decision taken now will shape the square for
decades, because reopening it after the rebuild will be hard. He also questioned the angled bays
proposed in combination with cycle traffic.

## The other argument is about hard ground

Around ten people, mostly returning from the July meeting, objected to the extent of the paved area
in the southern half. The brief had asked for three functions at once — street, park and square —
and the split in the room tracks that: some would minimise the square function, others ask for more
children's space, and the design gives most of the inner area to the park.

The constraint nobody can negotiate is the church. The brief required the undisturbed view of the
façade of Gočár's church of St Wenceslas, a composition the present sloped ground already spoils,
and that rules out shading the space in front of it with trees. The cooling has to come from
elsewhere: large-format stone paving, two rain gardens, retention tanks, roughly 25 cubic metres of
rootable space for a medium tree, rainwater harvested from the church roof instead of going to the
sewer, and a water feature whose regime changes with the weather — a few millimetres of water and
jets to paddle in on the hottest days.

Jury chair Martin Rusina reminded the room what stage this is. The competition design is a concept,
not, as the record puts it, scripture; Maršálek called it the zeroth phase of project preparation,
and the parking question explicitly remains open for the next stages. Which is why the straw poll
matters more than it looks: it is the first count of a Vršovice room asking for fewer cars than the
design in front of it.
