Dopravní podnik hl. m. Prahy held the ceremonial start of the Olšanská–Habrová tramway on 31
August, three days after the work itself began. On Saturday 29 August the contractors started
rebuilding the first part of the track junction at Olšanská and Jana Želivského, where the new line
will branch off the existing network.

The line is 1.9 kilometres long with six stops and a stub-end terminus at Jarov. It is costed at
CZK 599.2 million excluding VAT, co-funded by the European Union through the Transport Programme
2021–2027, and is being built by a consortium of FIRESTA – rekonstrukce, stavby; PEDASTA – dopravní
stavby; and N+N – Konstrukce a dopravní stavby Litoměřice. DPP gives the build twelve months and
expects services to start at the end of August 2027, with a direct tram journey to the centre of
about 15 minutes.

## The track arrives before the district does

For the first 450 metres past the junction, the tramway runs north of the retained freight-yard
building along the centre line of what will become the main street of the new Nákladové nádraží
Žižkov quarter, with two stops on it — Nákladové nádraží Žižkov and Bernasova. The street does not
exist yet. Housing is going up to the north and more starts within the next twelve months.

Jaromír Beránek, first deputy mayor for transport, framed the sequencing as the point of the
project:

> Bringing a high-capacity rail connection into a newly emerging quarter I consider an absolute
> necessity. Such a large part of the new city must be served above all by good public transport.
> It is therefore excellent news that we will manage to build the line in advance, at a moment when
> only a smaller part of the planned development stands on the former brownfield.
>
> — Jaromír Beránek, first deputy mayor of Prague for transport

The street profile was drawn by architects, and 68 honey locusts (*Gleditsia triacanthos*
'Shademaster') go in as two rows alongside the track; a third avenue comes with the neighbouring
developer's scheme. Root spaces get engineered substrates with biochar, and rainwater from the
carriageway, footways and stops is directed to the roots. Surplus goes into two retention basins of
25 and 40 cubic metres and is used to water the grassed track.

## Where the money went into restraint

Beyond Bernasova the double track passes under the K Červenému dvoru bridge, which TSK is replacing
in a parallel, coordinated investment. Třebešín stop follows, serving existing housing that
currently depends on buses, and a new traction substation, Červený dvůr, goes up nearby on Na
Viktorce, partly set into the slope. From Třebešín the line runs on the old railway alignment to
Vackov, near the supermarket, where the Y-516 footbridge over the former railway comes down and is
replaced by a level crossing for pedestrians and new footways. Along the railway section the track
is covered with sedum strips, chosen for lower maintenance and drought tolerance.

About 100 metres past Vackov the two tracks merge into one, used in both directions for roughly 600
metres to Habrová — a single island platform on the northern side — and on to the Jarov terminus by
Osiková, a two-track stub with an island platform and a new green-roofed staff facility.

That is not a cost saving for its own sake. The city's plan puts the future Jarovská třída and the
City Ring Road through the same corridor, and DPP says the timing of that construction is not known
but will certainly force larger changes. Building single track there is the cheapest possible way to
occupy the corridor until then; when the Jarovská třída is built, the section becomes double track
with a loop able to turn every tram type.

## The first one in forty-three years

Jan Šurovský, DPP board vice-chair and technical director for surface transport, called the
conversion unusual and singled out the single-track operation, which will be electronically
secured — cutting the power if two trams enter head-on.

> No single-track section has been operated in regular service in Prague since 1983, when the
> single track in Radlice disappeared.
>
> — Jan Šurovský, technical director – Surface, DPP

Prague 3's mayor Michal Vronský welcomed the investment and said the district would work with the
city and DPP to keep disruption down and information timely. Petr Hlaváček, deputy mayor for spatial
development, tied it to the Metropolitan Plan, arguing that large brownfields should be developed as
real neighbourhoods rather than as isolated projects.

This year's work is mostly groundworks: earthworks at the freight yard, utility diversions, new
storm drainage and the retention basins, then the track bed, feeder cables, catenary poles and,
last, the trees.
