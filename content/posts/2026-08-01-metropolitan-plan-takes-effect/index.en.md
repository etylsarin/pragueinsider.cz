On 1 September the Metropolitan Plan becomes the binding zoning instrument for Prague, ending a
drafting process that began when the city council voted to commission it in June 2012. Fourteen
years, two rounds of public comment and more than two hundred contributing specialists later, the
city replaces a plan whose underlying logic was formulated roughly a century ago.

## What actually changes

The outgoing plan inherits the interwar conviction that a city should be sorted into zones for
working, living and recreation. That principle has accumulated into a system of more than ninety
zone types — a document that is difficult to read even for the professionals who use it daily, and
correspondingly difficult to argue with in public.

The Metropolitan Plan swaps rigid function-by-parcel zoning for a framework built around the
character of localities. The practical difference shows up in how public amenities are handled:
rather than designating the exact parcel on which a school or a clinic must stand, the plan
specifies the function required and the accessibility it must have. That is a looser instrument,
and deliberately so.

## The number everyone will quote

The plan creates capacity for roughly 350,000 new apartments. The more consequential decision is
where it puts them. By concentrating that capacity on land already inside the city — brownfields,
railway lands, underused industrial sites — the plan is explicitly structured to stop Prague
spreading outward into open landscape, which is the pattern the city has followed for three
decades.

Transport reservations run through the document: the Praha-Sever high-speed rail terminus at
Letňany, a rail connection beneath the city centre, the extension of metro line D under Náměstí
Republiky, and a protected corridor for a future line E through Michle and Vršovice. None of these
are funded commitments. They are land held open so that funding them later remains possible, which
is the quieter and more durable thing a zoning plan does.

## Where the public got in

The first draft, published by the city's planning institute IPR in 2018, drew close to 46,000
individual submissions. A revised version followed in 2022 and attracted a further 18,000 comments,
most routed through the city's Portál Pražana. The final version was presented in autumn 2025.

> "I see the Metropolitan Plan above all as a service to the public."
>
> — Petr Hlaváček, Deputy Mayor of Prague for spatial and strategic development

One provision deserves more attention than it will get: the final version strengthens the role of
planning agreements, the contracts through which developers commit to contributing infrastructure
or public amenities in exchange for building rights. These are now required across 47 per cent of
transformation and development areas. That figure, more than the headline apartment count, will
determine what Prague actually receives from the next decade of construction.

## The deadline nobody set out to meet

The timing is not entirely voluntary. Under the new building act, Prague's existing zoning rules
would cease to apply on 31 December 2028. Without an adopted replacement the city would face a
regulatory vacuum, and with it the practical prospect of a construction freeze in the largest
building market in the country. The Metropolitan Plan arrives with more than two years to spare —
which, measured against the fourteen it took to write, is a narrower margin than it sounds.
