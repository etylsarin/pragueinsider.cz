There are more than 3,000 kilometres of vanished historical paths in Prague and its metropolitan
area, and a new study from the Institute of Planning and Development says a large share of them
could be brought back cheaply. IPR published the *Cesty* (Paths) study on 20 August. It examines the
scope for restoring lost and historical routes in the peri-urban landscape around Prague, and their
potential as green infrastructure and as a network for getting about without a car.

The number that makes the study actionable is not the 3,000 kilometres. It is the ownership: more
than 50 per cent of the vanished paths are still owned by municipalities today, with a further share
belonging to the state or the region.

## Why ownership is the whole argument

Land assembly is normally the thing that kills a new route through open country. IPR's framing is
that these particular routes escape that problem, because they never legally disappeared — they are
still entered in the cadastre.

> "Land in Prague and the surrounding area is owned by many owners, which complicates the delivery
> of any new paths or greenery. Our aim was first and foremost to map and examine the potential for
> restoring vanished paths that are still recorded in the cadastre and often still in municipal
> ownership, and which are therefore relatively easy to restore. That is why in June we presented
> the material at a meeting with the mayors of municipalities and city districts, including examples
> of completed schemes, with the aim of prompting further projects."
>
> — Marek Zděradička, Director of the Infrastructure Section, IPR Praha

That June presentation matters more than the publication date. The study is not a plan the city will
build; it is a catalogue handed to the people who would have to build it. Its concrete output is a
shortlist of priority sections suitable for restoration or adjustment.

## What a restored path is supposed to do

IPR describes the peri-urban landscape around Prague as suffering from fragmentation, a shortage of
vegetation and a gradual loss of the identity that the old paths carry. The study's case is that
low-cost measures against all three exist and are being under-used, even though some municipalities
and city districts are already restoring vanished paths on their own.

The claimed benefits are split between people and landscape. For people: better permeability of the
countryside, new recreational options, and links between the region and Prague for those who would
rather not drive. For the landscape: the vegetation along a path — tree alleys, shrubs, grass strips
— supports biodiversity, limits erosion, catches dust, damps noise and creates habitat.

IPR also puts the paths in the frame of climate change, growing traffic and continuing urbanisation,
which is a broader claim than the study's own mapping supports on its own, and is presented as
context rather than as a finding.

## The historical argument

Prague's position made it a trade and transport hub of European significance, and a network of
pilgrimage routes grew up around it — some heading for Prague Castle, others feeding the Way of St
James. The study notes that more than half of the original trading routes now coincide with the
present-day motorway network, because the historical paths took the ideal line through the terrain.

That is a neat observation with a sharp implication: the routes that were *not* absorbed into the
motorway network are exactly the ones still available. They are the candidates for recreation,
walking and cycling, and for making the landscape passable again.

## What would have to happen next

Zděradička's second point is about status rather than construction.

> "As already completed cases show, restoring paths need not be complicated — from mowing a strip of
> the path through surface treatment to planting an alley, you can gradually arrive at new paths in
> the landscape and a place for recreation. They must be seen as fully-fledged public
> infrastructure. They therefore need to be entered into plans, signage and street furniture added,
> and they need to be taken into account in area development and in transport projects."
>
> — Marek Zděradička, IPR Praha

Read together, the two quotes describe a gap rather than a project. The physical work is graded from
trivial (cut a strip) to modest (plant trees). The institutional work — getting a path into a plan,
onto a sign, and into the assumptions of a transport project — is the part nobody currently owns.
IPR has now given the municipalities a map and a priority list. Whether anything is cleared depends
on the mayors who sat in that room in June.
